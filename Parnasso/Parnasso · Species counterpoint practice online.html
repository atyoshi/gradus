    A step-by-step way of learning to write independent melodies that sound good together. Johann Joseph Fux set it out in 1725, and it moves from one note against one note to free, florid writing.

    How first species works
    • One note against each note of the cantus.
    • Every interval is a consonance: unison, 3rd, 5th, 6th or octave. A 4th counts as a dissonance.
    • Start on a perfect interval and end on a unison or octave.
    • No parallel 5ths or octaves, and reach them only by contrary motion.
    • Close with a 6th opening to an octave (or a 3rd closing to a unison below), raising the leading tone except in Phrygian.
    • Move mostly by step. Leap by 3rds, 4ths, 5ths, a rising minor 6th or an octave, then step back.

    Learn counterpoint

    Five species, each a small step up from the one before, as Johann Joseph Fux set them out in Gradus ad Parnassum (1725). Start with the basics: they hold in every species.

    Two lines, one given

    You get a fixed melody, the cantus firmus: whole notes, mostly by step, starting and ending on the final of its mode. You write a second line above or below it. The rules on this page apply in every species; each species then adds its own.

    Intervals

    Consonant and dissonant

    Unisons, 3rds, 5ths, 6ths and octaves are consonant, and so are the same intervals an octave higher (a 10th is a 3rd). 2nds, 4ths, 7ths and the tritone are dissonant. In two voices the 4th counts as a dissonance.

    Begin on a perfect consonance

    Above the cantus: unison, 5th or octave. Below it: unison or octave only, so the mode stays clear.

    End on a unison or octave

    And use the unison nowhere else: in the middle the two voices merge into one.

    The cadence

    Close with a major 6th opening to an octave (line above) or a minor 3rd closing to a unison (line below). In every mode except Phrygian this means raising the leading tone, here C to C♯.

    Motion between the voices

    No parallel 5ths or octaves

    Two perfect intervals of the same size in a row, with both voices moving, make the voices lose their independence. Parallel 3rds and 6ths are fine.

    No direct 5ths or octaves

    Approach a perfect interval by contrary or oblique motion, never with both voices moving the same way.

    Keep the voices apart and in order

    The upper line normally stays above. Fux lets the counterpoint cross the cantus for a moment when that avoids a worse progression, so Parnasso marks it as a suggestion. Keep the voices within a 10th where you can.

    The four rules of motion

    Perfect to perfect: contrary or oblique motion only. Perfect to imperfect: any motion. Imperfect to perfect: contrary motion only. Imperfect to imperfect: any motion. Oblique motion is allowed with every progression.

    The ottava battuta

    An octave reached on the downbeat from a 10th, with both voices stepping towards each other, is called the battuta. Fux says the use or avoidance is up to you: "it is of little importance". Parnasso does not mark it.

    Prefer contrary motion

    More than three 3rds or 6ths in a row, or too many bare 5ths and octaves, make the two lines sound like one.

    Shape of the line

    Move mostly by step

    Allowed leaps: 3rds, perfect 4ths and 5ths, the rising minor 6th and the octave. Avoid the tritone, 7ths and augmented intervals: they are hard to sing.

    Recover from leaps

    After a large leap, step back in the opposite direction. Avoid two leaps in the same direction.

    One climax

    Give the line one highest note, away from the cantus's high point, and keep its range singable: within a 10th.

    Don't outline a tritone

    When the line runs in one direction from F up to B (or down from B to F), the ear hears the tritone between its ends, even though every move is a step. Turn back before you reach it, or carry on past it.

    One repeated note is allowed

    The counterpoint may repeat a note occasionally, but the same note should not be repeated more than once (Fux). Parnasso suggests a change from the second repeat on.

    The modes

    Six church modes

    Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian and Ionian, on several finals each. Stay inside the mode. The only chromatic note is the raised leading tone at the cadence; Phrygian never raises it. Fux leaves out the mode on B: it has no perfect 5th above the final, so it cannot end a piece.

    More to learn9 notes
    • Count from the bass

      If the cantus is on top, count intervals down from it.

    • Compound intervals

      A 10th is a 3rd an octave higher. Write in neighbouring clefs so simple intervals stay easy to tell apart.

    • Why direct 5ths are banned

      Fill in a skip of a 5th with passing notes and two 5ths in a row appear.

    • Why perfect at the ends

      The start expresses perfection and the end relaxation. Imperfect consonances sound fuller, so they should outnumber the perfect ones.

    • Sharps and flats

      Only the leading tone at the cadence, and to avoid mi against fa. Never decoration.

    • Skips into a unison

      Poor, especially into a unison or into an octave from a distant consonance. Fux tolerates it only where the cantus forces it.

    • Stay in the mode

      The counterpoint begins and ends in the cantus's mode: opening on G over a D cantus forces it out.

    • Fux's study plan

      Every cantus in all six modes, line above and below, then the cantus in each voice. A year or two of practice before free composition.

    • Where cantus come from

      Fux gives no rule beyond singability: take a chorale or invent one.

    One line

    Writing a cantus firmus

    Before writing against a cantus, write one. A cantus firmus is a short, plain melody in whole notes: it sets out the mode, moves mostly by step and has a clear arc. Every species exercise starts from one.

    Begin and end on the final

    The first and last notes are the final of the mode, in the same octave. That is what makes the mode clear.

    Approach the final by step

    Usually from the note above. A step up from the leading tone below also works, but the cantus itself stays in the mode.

    Mostly steps, a few leaps

    One to three leaps give the line character. After a leap, step back the other way; never two leaps in a row.

    One climax, a singable range

    The highest note comes once, somewhere in the middle. Keep the whole line within about a 10th, with no repeated notes and nothing outside the mode.

    1 : 1

    First species

    One whole note against each note of the cantus. With no rhythm to hide behind, every interval counts. All the basics apply, plus two rules.

    Every interval is consonant

    No dissonances at all: each bar is a unison, 3rd, 5th, 6th or octave, or a compound of these.

    Avoid repeated notes

    With one note per bar, a repeated note stalls the line. Fux allows one repeat in the whole exercise, no more: move on, by step if you can.

    More to learn3 notes
    • The leading tone

      The 7th degree rises at the cadence in Dorian, Mixolydian and Aeolian. In Fux's cantus firmi the next to last note is the 2nd degree of the mode.

    • Number your intervals

      Write the interval over each note and the bar numbers below, to prove each progression is deliberate.

    • Contrary first

      Contrary and oblique motion make mistakes easier to avoid. Take more care with direct motion.

    2 : 1

    Second species

    Two half notes against each cantus note. The line opens with a half rest and ends on a whole note. The second half of a bar can now carry a dissonance, if it passes.

    Downbeats are consonant

    The first half note of every bar follows the first-species rules for harmony.

    Weak beats may pass

    The second half may be dissonant only as a passing tone: reached by step and left by step in the same direction, filling in a 3rd.

    Watch the downbeats

    5ths or octaves on two downbeats in a row are heard as parallels, even with a note between them. A leap of a 4th or more around that note makes the ear forget the first downbeat, so the parallel no longer counts.

    Three notes against one

    Fux mentions triple time only briefly: three notes can stand against one. If all three move by step, the middle one may be dissonant; if one of them leaps, all three must be consonant. Parnasso has no exercises for it.

    Keep moving

    No repeated notes. The cadence sits on the last half note: a major 6th above, or minor 3rd below, then the final.

    Avoid melodic sequences

    Fux asks to avoid sequences (monotonia) as far as possible, in every species from here on. A figure of two or three moves that is simply repeated again and again makes the line monotonous: change its shape the second time. Parnasso suggests it.

    More to learn5 notes
    • A skip to turn motion

      A skip of a minor 6th or an octave can turn a direct approach into a contrary one.

    • A whole note near the end

      It may stand in the next to last bar when two half notes cannot reach the cadence.

    • Phrygian cadence

      The 5th is dissonant, so write a 6th in the next to last bar instead.

    • Plan the ending first

      It is the hardest bar. Look one or two bars ahead before you write.

    • A half rest to open

      The line may start with a half rest instead of a half note.

    4 : 1

    Third species

    Four quarter notes against each cantus note, opening with a quarter rest. The downbeat is consonant; the other three quarters may be dissonant, handled in one of three ways.

    Passing tone

    On any of the three weak quarters: reached and left by step in the same direction.

    Neighbour note

    On the 2nd or 4th quarter: a step away from a consonance and straight back to it.

    The cambiata

    On the 2nd quarter: step down to the dissonance, leap a 3rd down to a consonance, then step up. The one dissonance in species counterpoint that is left by leap.

    The rest still holds

    No repeated notes, no parallels across the barline, no 5ths or octaves on successive downbeats.

    More to learn6 notes
    • The five-quarter rule

      1st and 3rd consonant; the 2nd may be dissonant; the 4th only if the 5th is consonant.

    • Filling a 3rd

      If the 2nd and 4th quarters are consonant, the 3rd may be dissonant: it only fills in a 3rd.

    • Rests and eighths

      A quarter rest may open the bar. No eighths yet: they belong to fifth species.

    • A 7th on the 2nd quarter

      Fux accepts it in one old model because it is easier to sing. Most teachers avoid it.

    • A later rule

      A leap from a strong to a weak quarter is hard to sing in the Palestrina style (Jeppesen). This is not Fux's rule.

    • Plan the next bar

      Avoid melodic sequences, and plan the next bar before you write.

    Ties

    Fourth species

    Half notes, shifted: each note is struck on the second half of the bar and tied over the barline into the next downbeat. When the cantus moves under the tie, you get a suspension.

    The struck note is consonant

    It resolves the last suspension and prepares the next one.

    A dissonant tie resolves down by step

    Above the cantus use 7–6, 4–3, 9–8 or 2–1. Below the cantus use 2–3. A 9–8 resolves onto a bare octave, so 7–6 and 4–3 are preferred.

    Not after a unison or an octave

    Fux forbids a 2–1 suspension that comes after a unison, and a 9–8 after an octave. Take the tie away and you hear two unisons or two octaves in a row. A 3rd or 10th leading into the 2nd or 9th is fine.

    A tie in every bar you can

    Fux asks for a tie in every bar where one is possible. Where none works, write plain half notes until a tie fits again, and never repeat the same note. The same ties may also be varied: two eighths on the 2nd and 4th beats, or the note struck again, keep the idea but liven the line.

    A consonant tie is free

    If the tied note is consonant against the new cantus note, the next note may step or leap.

    The cadence

    The classic 7–6 above (2–3 below), resolving to the octave or unison, with the leading tone raised.

    Breaking the species

    Sometimes no tie leads anywhere good: every suspension would make parallels or an unresolvable dissonance. Then Fux breaks the chain for one bar: the note is not tied, and the next bar starts with a fresh, consonant note, as in second species. Use it once at most. In Parnasso, select the note and press + to break or restore its tie.

    More to learn5 notes
    • Resolve downwards

      Fux leaves the case of an ascending tie for later.

    • No 7th with the cantus on top

      It would resolve to an octave, which the masters never wrote.

    • Vary the tie figure

      Avoid repeating the same tie figure bar after bar.

    • Pedal points

      In three voices a tie over a pedal point may be dissonant throughout.

    • No sequences

      Avoid sequences as far as possible.

    Florid

    Fifth species

    All the species mixed, bar by bar: halves, quarters, a pair of eighths, and ties that make suspensions. Choose a rhythm for each bar, then write the notes.

    Each note keeps its own rule

    Struck downbeats are consonant. Dissonant halves pass; dissonant quarters pass, neighbour or form a cambiata; dissonant ties resolve down by step.

    Eighths move by step

    In and out, usually as a pair on the 2nd or 4th quarter of the bar.

    Decorating a resolution

    In florid counterpoint a suspension may be ornamented before it resolves: short consonant notes after the tie, most often a 3rd down and back up, as long as the step down arrives by beat 4.

    Dotted notes

    A dotted quarter and an eighth, or a dotted half and a quarter, give the line a lilt. Dotted notes start on a beat; the eighth after a dotted quarter moves by step. Press . to dot the selected note.

    Vary the rhythm

    The same rhythm three bars in a row sounds like a lower species. Let the line speed up and slow down.

    Reach the downbeat by a tie or oblique motion

    In florid counterpoint Fux likes to reach each downbeat with a tie or by oblique motion: it gives the greatest beauty and keeps the line out of trouble. A singable melodic line matters more than anything else in this species, and Fux tells his student to spend most time here.

    Two quarters, then a plain half

    Fux advises against two quarters at the start of a bar followed by a half note that is not tied on: the line seems to lag. Tie the half over the barline, or add more quarters. Parnasso suggests it.

    More to learn4 notes
    • A rest for a note

      A rest may stand in place of a note when the line has nowhere good to go.

    • Watch the downbeat

      Every struck downbeat note is consonant.

    • The most valuable species

      Fux says to work at it more than at any other.

    • Singable first

      Mix the other species freely, but let the line stay singable.

    3–4 voices

    Three and four voices

    After the five species in two voices, Fux adds voices. The cantus may sit in the bass, the middle or on top; the other voices move note against note, or one of them in a higher species. Every rule still holds between every pair of voices, and each chord is now heard from the bass.

    Hear every chord from the bass

    A 4th above the bass is a dissonance. Between two upper voices it is fine: in C–G–C the G and C make a 4th, but each is consonant with the bass.

    Full chords

    In the middle of the exercise, give each chord a 3rd (or a 6th) above the bass. Don't double the raised leading tone. A tritone between upper voices is allowed but tense.

    The 6th over mi in the bass

    When the bass has the third degree of the mode (B in Dorian, the "mi"), put a 6th above it, not a 5th: the 5th would be diminished. A 3rd and a 6th make the chord.

    Spread and double

    Put the 5th low and the 3rd high: a 3rd close to the bass sounds dull (Fux). Voices that sit close together sound fuller. Double the octave first; if a progression forbids that, double the 3rd, more rarely the 5th or 6th. In a suspension double the 2nd, not the 4th.

    Build the triad first

    On each downbeat use the full triad, a 3rd and a 5th above the bass, unless the melody or a parallel forbids it. Then take a 6th or an octave instead of the 5th. Prefer the version in which every voice moves smoothly and no note is doubled for no reason.

    Full downbeats, lighter upbeats

    The downbeat should carry a full chord; the upbeat may be thinner. In ties and quarters, give the note against the whole notes the consonances it would have if the tie were taken away.

    Parallels between every pair

    No parallel 5ths or octaves between any two voices. Direct 5ths and octaves matter most between the outer voices, when the top voice leaps.

    First and last chords

    The bass stands on the final. Above it: the octave and the 5th. Leave out the 3rd when you can; if the 5th would make parallels, take a major 3rd, never a minor one, which Fux finds too weak to end on.

    The cadence

    One voice rises from the raised leading tone to the final, as in two voices; when the bass is free, it often leaps from the 5th to the final.

    A little more freedom

    With more voices, a repeated note, a unison or a brief crossing is sometimes the best way through, and a hidden 5th or octave is tolerated between inner voices when the voice leading is otherwise good, more than between the outer ones. Use them sparingly; Parnasso marks them as suggestions, not errors. The more voices, the looser the rules.

    One voice in a higher species

    When one voice moves in halves, quarters, ties or florid rhythm, it follows that species' rules against every other voice; the rest move note against note.

    More to learn13 notes
    • Rotate the cantus

      Practise with the cantus in the top, the middle and the bottom voice.

    • Five lines

      Stay within the five lines of the staff and prefer an octave to a unison.

    • Diminished 5th

      It is wrong between any two voices, not only against the bass.

    • Half notes

      One voice moves in halves and the others in wholes; each half note must be consonant with both whole notes.

    • A skip excuses 5ths

      A half note may excuse two parallel 5ths by a skip of a 3rd, if that keeps the triad.

    • A rest hides a 5th

      A rest in one voice can hide a hidden 5th between the others.

    • Quarter exercises

      Give the first quarter the triad. If that fails, use the 2nd or 3rd quarter.

    • Four voices and unisons

      Double the 3rd rather than writing a unison on a downbeat.

    • A 4th inside a tie

      A 4th between inner voices acts like an imperfect consonance.

    • Dividing a whole note

      Allowed in an accompanying voice when no other solution exists; Fux avoids it where he can.

    • Down, not up

      A direct move into a perfect interval is easier to accept descending than ascending (Beethoven).

    • Phrygian is hardest

      With no perfect 5th, the final chord and cadence need a different solution.

    • Beyond four voices

      The rules loosen further. Fux ends the book here.

    About Parnasso

    A practice room for composers who learn on their own and have no teacher to mark their exercises.

    Every exercise is short: a cantus firmus and a line to write against it. The rules of counterpoint are checked by code, so a parallel fifth is always caught and never guessed. On top of that, Parnasso comments on the shape of your line: its climax, its leaps, how independent it is from the cantus.

    The name comes from Mount Parnassus, home of the Muses. In 1725 Johann Joseph Fux called his counterpoint textbook Gradus ad Parnassum, "steps to Parnassus": a climb toward mastery, one exercise at a time. That book taught Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven. Parnasso follows the same path, starting with counterpoint and climbing toward harmony and beyond.

    1. 1725Johann Joseph Fux publishes Gradus ad Parnassum, “steps to Parnassus”.
    2. AfterThe book trains composers including Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven.
    3. NowParnasso checks every rule of that method as you write, and explains it.

          Privacy

          Parnasso collects as little as it can. No ads, no tracking cookies, no selling of data.

          What is stored in your browser

          Your current exercise, preferences and record are kept in your browser's local storage, along with a random ID used for anonymous usage counts. You can clear them at any time by clearing this site's data.

          What is stored on our servers

          • If you sign in: your email address (and, if you continue with Google, the name and profile picture Google shares with us; we use them only to set up your account), the exercises you have solved and your current exercise, so they follow you to other devices.
          • If you join a list (the Solo or Teacher lists, or MusicXML downloads): your email address and which list you joined.
          • If you are in a class: your name and email as your teacher entered them, and your work on the class's assignments. Your teacher can see that work and leave you notes; no one else can. Leaving the class deletes your work in it.
          • Challenge links: the exercise in the link and how many times it was opened and solved. Not who opened it.
          • Usage counts: anonymous events such as "exercise solved" or "image downloaded", tied to the random browser ID, never to your email.
          • Messages you send through the contact form.

          Who processes it

          Data is stored with Supabase in the European Union (Frankfurt). The site is served by Cloudflare. Sign-in emails are sent through our email provider.
